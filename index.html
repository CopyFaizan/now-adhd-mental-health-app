🧠 Cognitive Distortions

From David Burns' CBT workbook. Spot the pattern → break the loop.

Why this matters for ADHD: ADHD brains are more prone to emotional reasoning, all-or-nothing thinking, and labeling ("I'm broken", "I always fail"). Naming the distortion breaks its power.
1
All-or-Nothing Thinking
›
You see things in absolute black-and-white. If a situation falls short of perfect, you see it as a total failure.
ADHD version: "I missed one task — the whole day is ruined." "I got distracted once — I can never focus." Fuels task paralysis and shame spirals.
2
Overgeneralization
›
You view a single negative event as a never-ending pattern using words like "always" or "never."
ADHD version: "I always forget things." "I never finish what I start." Each instance feels like proof of a permanent truth.
3
Mental Filter
›
You pick one negative detail and dwell on it exclusively, like a drop of ink darkening a whole glass of water.
ADHD version: Got 9 things right, 1 wrong. You obsess over the 1 for the rest of the day while ignoring all 9 wins.
4
Discounting the Positives
›
You reject positive experiences by insisting they "don't count."
ADHD version: "I only finished because I set 10 alarms — that doesn't count." You dismiss every coping tool as cheating.
5
Jumping to Conclusions
›
Mind reading (assuming what others think) or fortune telling (predicting bad outcomes) without real evidence.
ADHD version: "They didn't reply — they're annoyed at me." (RSD classic.) "I'll definitely bomb this." You catastrophize before any evidence exists.
6
Magnification or Minimization
›
You catastrophize problems or shrink strengths. The "binocular trick" — always the wrong lens.
ADHD version: A small mistake becomes catastrophic. A big achievement gets: "it wasn't that hard." Always distorted.
7
Emotional Reasoning
›
You assume your negative emotions reflect reality. "I feel it, therefore it must be true."
ADHD version: "I feel stupid — so I must be stupid." ADHD emotional dysregulation (RSD) makes this extremely powerful and painful.
8
"Should" Statements
›
You criticize yourself with "shoulds," "shouldn'ts," "musts," "oughts." Creates guilt, shame, and rebellion.
ADHD version: "I should be able to focus like everyone else." "I shouldn't need reminders." Creates the shame spiral that makes ADHD worse.
9
Labeling
›
Instead of "I made a mistake" you say "I'm a jerk," "a fool," "a loser." You are not the same as what you do.
ADHD version: "I'm broken." "I'm lazy." "I'm stupid." These feel like facts — they're not. They're the most damaging thought pattern for ADHD self-esteem.
10
Personalization & Blame
›
You hold yourself responsible for events not entirely under your control — or blame others and overlook your own role.
ADHD version: "My team failed because of my ADHD." Or the flip: "Nothing is ever my fault — the world doesn't accommodate me." Both block growth.
10 techniques directly from Burns' CBT workbook. Each dismantles a distorted thought.

1. Identify the Distortion

Write the thought, then name the distortion. This single step creates distance — and distance creates freedom.

2. Examine the Evidence

List actual evidence for and against the thought. Real data, not feelings. What have you actually done successfully?

3. Double-Standard Method

Talk to yourself in the same compassionate way you'd talk to a friend with the same problem. Would you say this to them?

4. The Experimental Technique

Run an experiment to test your negative belief. Afraid your heart is unhealthy? Go jog. Let data replace fear.

5. Shades of Gray

Rate things on 0–100 instead of pass/fail. A partial success is not a total failure. What % did you succeed today?

6. The Survey Method

Ask others if your thought is realistic. Do your friends also feel nervous before talks? Do they also forget things?

7. Define Your Terms

When you label yourself "a fool" — define what that actually means. There is no universal "fool." The label collapses under examination.

8. The Semantic Method

Replace "I should exercise" with "I could exercise." Remove the punishment. Replace coercion with invitation.

9. Re-attribution

List every factor that contributed to a problem — not just you. Distribute responsibility accurately, not entirely on yourself.

10. Cost–Benefit Analysis

How does believing this thought help you? How does it hurt you? This replaces guilt with a clear-eyed choice.

🛡️ DBT Skills

Distress tolerance from Dialectical Behaviour Therapy + social anxiety tools.


Radical Acceptance

When a problem is out of your control, fighting it only adds pain. Radical acceptance means recognizing and accepting the situation as it is — not liking it, just not fighting it.

Typical thinking"This isn't fair. I shouldn't have this problem. Why does this always happen to me?"
Radical acceptance"It's frustrating, and I accept it's happening. Now — what can I actually do?"
ADHD note: Acceptance cuts the shame spiral. You can't focus better by hating that you can't focus.

ACCEPTS — Distress Distraction

When emotions are overwhelming, distraction isn't avoidance — it's survival. A.C.C.E.P.T.S. gives you a menu of ways to ride out a wave until it passes.

Self-Soothe with Senses

Find a pleasurable way to engage each sense. Positive sensory input directly calms the nervous system — no thinking required.

ADHD note: Sensory seeking is common in ADHD. Use it intentionally — a warm drink, a walk, music you love.

Safety Behaviors & Social Anxiety

Safety behaviors are subtle actions (avoiding eye contact, speaking softly, rehearsing scripts, using your phone as a shield) that provide short-term relief but maintain — and increase — anxiety long-term.

The cycle: Anxiety → Safety behavior → Temporary relief → Anxiety grows next time.

ADHD + RSD version: Masking, over-explaining, people-pleasing, and interrupting to keep control are all safety behaviors driven by fear of rejection.

📖 The Power of Now

Core principles from Eckhart Tolle — adapted for ADHD minds.


The Now is All You Have

Your ADHD mind races between past regrets and future worries — but life only happens now. This moment is your only real access point to peace and action.

You Are Not Your Thoughts

The endless mental chatter is not "you." You are the awareness watching those thoughts. When you can observe a thought, you are already free from it.

The Pain Body

We accumulate emotional pain that lives in the body. For ADHD brains, this often feels like restlessness, emotional dysregulation, or constant tension. Awareness begins to dissolve it.

Acceptance Over Resistance

What you resist, persists. Acceptance means: "This is here. Now what do I do?" Then action becomes clear instead of paralysed.

The Ego Creates Suffering

The ego needs drama, needs to be right, compares constantly. For ADHD minds, this shows up as self-criticism and shame spirals. Seeing the ego is the first step out.

Body = Your Portal to Now

When your mind is out of control, your body is the shortcut back. Breath, sensations, movement — these only happen now. Always free, always available.

Tolle + CBT + DBT together: Tolle says "you are not your thoughts." Burns says "your thoughts are distorted — examine them." DBT says "when it's unbearable, survive the wave." All three together = a complete ADHD toolkit.
